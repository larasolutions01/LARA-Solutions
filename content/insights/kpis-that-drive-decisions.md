It’s easy to build a dashboard. It’s much harder to build one that changes what people do on Monday morning.

The difference usually comes down to how the metrics were chosen.

## Start from decisions, not data

Instead of asking *“What data do we have?”*, ask *“What decisions do we make regularly, and what would help us make them better?”*

For example:

- **Decision:** Where should we spend next month’s marketing budget? → **KPI:** cost per qualified opportunity by channel.
- **Decision:** Do we need to hire more support staff? → **KPI:** ticket volume and resolution time trends.
- **Decision:** Which products should we reorder this week? → **KPI:** days of stock cover against forecast demand.

## Apply three tests

A good KPI is:

1. **Actionable** — someone can do something about it.
2. **Owned** — a named person is responsible for it.
3. **Timely** — it arrives while there’s still time to act.

If a metric fails any of these tests, it may still be interesting, but it doesn’t belong on the main dashboard.

## Balance leading and lagging indicators

Revenue and profit are **lagging** indicators — they tell you what already happened. Pair them with **leading** indicators that move earlier, such as pipeline created, website enquiries or order backlog.

## Make definitions explicit

Write down exactly how each KPI is calculated. When different teams calculate “active customer” differently, trust in the numbers erodes quickly.

## Build the habit

Finally, connect the dashboard to a meeting rhythm. A short weekly review — what changed, why, and what we’ll do — turns data into decisions.

Fewer, better KPIs, clearly owned and regularly reviewed, will do more for your business than the most elaborate dashboard ever built.
