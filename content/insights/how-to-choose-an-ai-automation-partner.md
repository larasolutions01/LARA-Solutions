Choosing who to work with on AI matters as much as choosing what to build. The right partner shortens the path to value and helps you avoid expensive detours; the wrong one can leave you with an impressive demo that nobody uses.

These ten questions will help you compare agencies, consultants and freelancers on what actually predicts success. (We’re an AI automation agency ourselves, so we’ve tried to write the questions we would want a client to ask *us*.)

## Business understanding

**1. What questions do they ask before proposing anything?**
A strong partner wants to understand your goals, customers, processes and constraints before recommending technology. Be cautious if the first meeting is mainly a product demo.

**2. Can they explain where AI is *not* the right answer?**
Some problems are better solved by fixing a process, improving data or buying an existing tool. A partner who can say “you don’t need AI for this” is more likely to be honest elsewhere. See our view on [fixing the process before you automate](/insights/fix-the-process-before-you-automate).

**3. How will success be measured?**
Ask how they will establish a baseline — time per task, error rates, response times, conversion — and what measures each phase will be judged on. Be wary of promised outcomes that aren’t tied to your own numbers.

## Delivery and risk

**4. How do they handle data, privacy and security?**
Ask specifically: what data the system will access, where it is processed and stored, who can see it, and how access is controlled and logged. Ask which AI services they use and what those services do with your data.

**5. What happens when the AI gets something wrong?**
Every AI system makes mistakes. A good answer covers human review for sensitive steps, confidence thresholds, escalation paths and logging so issues can be traced and fixed.

**6. How do they think about AI risk and governance?**
They don’t need a large compliance team, but they should be able to describe how they identify risks, test systems and assign accountability. Public frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and the OECD AI Principles are useful references for this conversation.

**7. Will they start small?**
A focused pilot with clear success measures lowers your risk and proves value before larger investment. Be cautious about proposals that require a large commitment before anything has been tested in your environment.

## After launch

**8. Who owns what?**
Clarify ownership of code, workflows, prompts, data and accounts. You should be able to continue — or switch partners — without starting again.

**9. What does support look like after go-live?**
AI systems need monitoring: models change, data drifts and processes evolve. Ask what ongoing support includes, how issues are handled and what running costs to expect.

**10. Will your team be able to use and understand it?**
Adoption decides whether a system delivers value. Look for training, documentation and a plan for the people whose work is changing.

## Red flags worth noticing

- Guaranteed results or specific savings promised before they understand your business.
- Vague answers about where your data goes.
- No mention of human review, testing or monitoring.
- Pressure to commit to a large programme up front.
- Case studies or testimonials that can’t be explained in any detail.

## Putting it into practice

Use the questions as a scorecard when you speak to potential partners, and weigh the quality of the answers — specific, honest and tied to your business — over the polish of the presentation.
