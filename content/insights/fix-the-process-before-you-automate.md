There’s an old rule in operations: automating an inefficient process gives you an inefficient process that runs faster. It remains one of the most common reasons automation projects disappoint.

## What a process map reveals

Mapping a process simply means writing down, step by step, how a piece of work moves from start to finish — who touches it, which tools are involved, and where it waits.

When teams do this honestly, the same patterns appear again and again:

- **Hand-offs** where work sits in someone’s inbox waiting for attention.
- **Duplicate entry**, where the same information is typed into two or three systems.
- **Approval loops** that exist because of a problem that happened years ago.
- **Workarounds** — the spreadsheet that “everyone knows about” but nobody owns.

## A simple afternoon exercise

1. Choose one process with a clear start and end, such as *enquiry to quote* or *order to invoice*.
2. Invite the people who actually do the work, not just those who manage it.
3. List each step on a sticky note or whiteboard, including waits and rework.
4. Mark each step: does it **add value** for the customer, is it **necessary** for the business, or is it **waste**?
5. Circle the two or three biggest delays.

## Then decide what to automate

With the map in front of you, the right automation opportunities become obvious. Often they’re smaller and more targeted than expected: removing one re-keying step, routing work automatically, or generating a document that is currently written from scratch every time.

Some improvements need no technology at all — removing an unnecessary approval, or agreeing a standard template.

## Measure before and after

Record how long the process takes today and where it stalls. After changes, measure again. This keeps the conversation grounded in results and helps you decide where to invest next.

## The payoff

Process-first automation is less glamorous than starting with the latest tool, but it is far more likely to deliver lasting improvement. Technology should serve a good process — not hide a bad one.
